Spot the phish

Phishing is a message that pretends to be someone you trust so you will click, reply, or hand over information. Below is a fake email. Click each highlighted part to see why it is a red flag. There are five to find.

From:
To: undisclosed-recipients
Subject:

We noticed unusual activity on your account. To avoid permanent closure, confirm your details now:

(links to http://paypa1-secure.com/login)

Details are also in the attached file:

Thank you,
Security Team

Recognize fake websites

A phishing link often leads to a copy of a real login page. The page looks right, so check the address. The part that matters is the domain, which sits just before the first single /.

Web addressVerdictWhy
accounts.google.comGenuineThe domain is google.com. The part before it is a normal subdomain.
accounts.google.com.secure-login.netFakeThe real domain is secure-login.net. Google's name is only a decoy at the front.
paypa1.comFakeThe letter "l" is replaced by the number "1". Lookalike characters are common.
myaccount.google.com-securitysettingpage.tkFakeA real 2016 phishing address. The domain ends in .tk, and "google.com-" is only part of a longer name.
bit.ly/3xYzHiddenShortened links hide the destination. Do not enter a password after clicking one from an email.

The padlock icon only means the connection is encrypted. Criminals can get a padlock for a fake site too, so it does not prove the site is safe.

Social engineering: the tricks behind the message

Attackers rarely break technology first. They manipulate people. These are the pressures they use most.

Urgency

"Act within 24 hours." Rushing stops you from checking. A real organization gives you time.

Authority

The message seems to come from your boss, bank, or IT team. Attackers borrow trusted names.

Fear

"Your account was hacked" or "you will be fined." Panic makes people click first.

Reward

Prizes, refunds, or free gifts. If it sounds too good to be true, it is bait.

Familiarity

Attackers study you on social media, then mention real names or events to sound genuine. This is spear phishing.

Other channels

Smishing is phishing by text message. Vishing is by phone call. Attackers also use fake invoices and fake job offers.

Real-world cases

These attacks happened to large, well-run organizations. Anyone can be fooled when the trick is good and the person is busy.

Twitter, July 2020

Attackers phoned a small number of Twitter employees in a phone spear-phishing attack and got access to internal tools. They targeted 130 accounts and posted a Bitcoin scam from 45 of them, including famous people. Victims sent over $100,000.

Lesson: verify unexpected calls using a number you look up yourself.

Google and Facebook, 2013 to 2015

A man set up a company with the same name as a real hardware supplier, Quanta Computer, and emailed forged invoices. Google paid about $23 million and Facebook about $98 million before the fraud was found. He pleaded guilty in 2019.

Lesson: confirm invoices and bank-detail changes by calling a known contact.

Fake Google alert, March 2016

A senior official on a US political campaign received a fake Google security alert with a shortened link to a fake sign-in page. Security researchers linked the attack to the group known as Fancy Bear. A staff member asked to check it replied that the alert was legitimate, which was wrong.

Lesson: never use the link in the alert. Go to the official site yourself.

Best practices

Five seconds of checking beats days of damage.

  • Pause. Urgent messages are the most suspicious ones.
  • Check the sender's domain, not just the display name.
  • Hover over links before clicking. On a phone, press and hold.
  • Go direct. Type the website address yourself or use your saved bookmark.
  • Never share passwords, PINs, or one-time codes with anyone who contacts you.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication for email, banking, and social media.
  • Use a password manager so unique passwords are easy. It also will not fill a fake site.
  • Do not open unexpected attachments, especially .zip, .exe, or Office files asking to enable macros.
  • Verify through a second channel for money or sensitive requests: call a number you already know.
  • Report it. Forward to your IT or security team, then delete. If you clicked, change your password at once from the official site.
